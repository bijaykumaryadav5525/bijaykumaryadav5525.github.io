Case Study 4.1: SolarWinds Supply Chain Attack

Risk, Crisis and Security Management (CC5052)
18,000+ Organizations Affected U.S. Government Agencies SUNBURST / Solorigate
📋 4.1.1 Background

SolarWinds is a prominent American IT technology company specializing in software for IT management and monitoring. Established in 1999 and headquartered in Austin, Texas, the company has assisted organizations in effectively managing their systems, network configurations, applications, and IT infrastructures.

The SolarWinds hack was particularly serious because it constituted a much larger supply chain attack that impacted over 18,000 organizations, including multiple U.S. government agencies. Unlike typical breaches where a single company is hacked, this attack leveraged SolarWinds' trusted software update mechanism to compromise thousands of downstream customers simultaneously.

⏱️ 4.1.2 SolarWinds Attack Timeline
1
1999
Company Origin
SolarWinds was established in Austin, Texas, focusing on cost-effective IT monitoring tools for system and network infrastructures. The company initially aimed to create easy-to-implement, cost-effective solutions for IT management.
2
2010
Orion Platform Launch
SolarWinds introduced the Orion platform as the foundation for its monitoring products. Following its Initial Public Offering (IPO) in 2010, the company gained worldwide recognition and significantly expanded its client base across multiple industries.
3
October 2019
Malware Introduced (SUNBURST)
Attackers inserted the first known malicious code into the Orion software development environment. This code, designed to appear like legitimate update files, became the backdoor known as SUNBURST (also called Solorigate). The attacker gained initial foothold through compromised credentials.
4
March 2020
Malicious Update Released
The first trojanized SolarWinds Orion update (version 2019.4) was released to users. The update was digitally signed with SolarWinds' official certificate and contained the SUNBURST backdoor. The signature made the malicious update appear completely legitimate to security systems.
5
June – September 2020
Supply Chain Attack Propagation
Thousands of companies worldwide received the malicious updates as they were distributed through normal automatic update channels. The attack spread rapidly across government agencies, technology companies, and critical infrastructure providers.
6
November 2020
Attack Discovered
FireEye, a cybersecurity firm, observed unusual activity on its own network. After launching an internal investigation, FireEye identified the SolarWinds Orion update as the source of the compromise, triggering a global response from governments and private organizations.

⚠️ 4.1.3 Issues Identifications
🔐 Issue 1: Identity and Access Management Failures

In the context of managing cyber risk, identity and access management is essential. Attackers claimed to be authenticating users and systems using compromised log credentials that had been accessed.

Key Failures:

  • Weak password policies
  • Lack of authority separation
  • Insufficient supervision of privileged accounts

Risk Implication: Once attackers obtained valid credentials, they were able to move laterally within systems without triggering alarms.

Risk Level: HIGH
📊 Issue 2: Insufficient Current Risk Monitoring

Risk monitoring entails keeping an eye on abnormal activity within a system. The fact that attackers went months without being identified demonstrates critical gaps.

Missing Controls:

  • Threat data integration
  • Anomaly detection mechanisms
  • Comprehensive logging and alerting

Risk Implication: Organizations missed the opportunity to mitigate both impact and likelihood because early warning signs were ignored. The dwell time of 8-9 months allowed extensive damage.

Risk Level: CRITICAL
🔏 Issue 3: Inadequate Software Verification Controls

Software updates were digitally encrypted and originated from an authorized source, leading to implicit trust. Critical verification steps were missing.

Missing Controls:

  • Third-party verification
  • Virtual testing (sandboxing)
  • Physical analysis of updates before deployment

Risk Implication: Malicious code was developed internally by attackers who gained full access due to weak control measures. Malicious updates represent a high-impact risk that was not mitigated.

Risk Level: CRITICAL
🛡️ Issue 4: Lack of a Zero-Trust Risk System

The SolarWinds attack succeeded primarily because both SolarWinds and its victims operated on a model of implicit trust rather than Zero-Trust architecture.

Key Deficiencies:

  • No continuous verification: Once inside the network, attackers were trusted.
  • No micro-segmentation: Compromised servers could communicate freely with high-value targets.
  • No least privilege enforcement: Service accounts had excessive privileges.
  • No real-time monitoring: Anomalous DNS traffic went undetected for months.

Risk Implication: A Zero-Trust architecture ("never trust, always verify") would have significantly reduced the attack's impact.

Risk Level: CRITICAL
